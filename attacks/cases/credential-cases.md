# Step 2: Case evidence for credential acquisition paths and security conditions

This file organizes the public literature according to §3 of the execution entry. The research has two purposes. It supplies facts about material acquisition for the impersonation analysis. It supplies real-world conditions and counterexamples for the per-paper assumption checks. **This round only screened the registers and verified public sources. It did not obtain or use credentials, run payloads, access leaked originals or reproduce anything. It did not derive new paths paper by paper or check this thesis's defences.**

Thesis tasks and delivery status belong to TASKS. This file is one evidence delivery, not a new status queue. The old packets' raw files, source levels, fragments and approval states were not modified.

## 0. Overview of case results

This file keeps the screening, the full cases, the condition index, the original source locations and the evidence limits in one place, for steps 3/5 to select from paper by paper. B1–B19 contain material acquisition, condition references, alternates, negative controls and restricted facts from collections. BM1 and BM2 are official records of mechanism defects. They cannot be added up into a homogeneous count of successful attacks.

| Research direction | Cases | Evidence conclusion and boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **Credential protection: two leak paths** | **Induced disclosure:** Cursor tool poisoning, Comet, Supabase MCP (B1–B3); **no disclosure by the victim agent needed:** OpenClaw infostealer, Nx, Sysdig (B4–B6); **negative control:** ElizaOS (B9) | Both paths have representatives. Supabase uses fictitious tokens. Execution credentials such as service accounts are relevant protected objects. Their misuse is judged separately from misidentification of a local agent's identity. Nx/Sysdig have reports of later credential use. The other records cannot be filled in as completed impersonation. ElizaOS shows that deceived proxy action can still happen while secret isolation holds. |
| **Correct binding** | Vault AWS, Vault GCP (B18–B19); SPIRE PID reuse (BM1); Docker Sandboxes official defect fixes (BM2); Amazon Q (B7) and SPIRE after node root (BN-05) as boundary references | The Vault records support mis-issuance of identity capability under specific conditions. The PID record supports that the caller lifecycle once had a security defect, but there is no public full impersonation trace. The Docker record supports that a deployed sandbox credential proxy once had defects in cross-sandbox login hijacking, component trust inheritance, name reuse and revocation taking effect. All were fixed, with no exploitation trace. None of this establishes that, under a trusted node, a restricted agent has impersonated another non-colluding agent. |
| **Complete mediation** | Cowork, ForcedLeak, Copilot, CurXecute, MCPoison, the restricted stage of OpenAI/Hugging Face, Claude Code, Gemini, Zed (B8, B10–B17); Amazon Q (B7) provides a reference for identity-context inheritance | They cover recipients, domain control, approval policy, the time of approval and its continued link to content, auxiliary services, consumers outside isolation, and run modes. They are condition evidence. They are not credential-leak evidence, and they do not directly prove that any paper fails or that this thesis can defend. |

**Classification conventions:** P1 = the victim agent is induced to disclose; P2 = material acquisition does not depend on disclosure by the victim agent; for mixed chains, the performer of each part is recorded separately. normal only means not actively colluding; it does not mean always correct. Users, service accounts, devices, gateways and agent instances are different identity granularities. Material acquisition, valid credential, identity accepted, legitimate delegation and deceived proxy action are judged separately.

The cases support the existence of specific paths or conditions. Per-paper applicability, whether this thesis establishes the conditions or can defend, and whether existing combinations have the same effect each still need their own evidence. K1–K18 are entry points for checks. They are not a presumption that every paper depends on them, nor guarantees this thesis already provides.

## A. Full shallow screening and deduplication

Cases were screened by whether the specific path/condition is relevant and whether the evidence is sufficient: **recorded in detail** (full fields in §B, including condition references/alternates), **related alternate**, **mechanism reference**, **to be verified**, **out of current scope**. At present 24 old register entries are recorded in detail, 23 are related alternates, 3 are mechanism references, 9 are to be verified and 12 are out of current scope, 71 in total. Duplicates across packets cannot be counted as independent incidents.

An endpoint without credentials only limits the use for leaks. On its own it cannot rule out use for assumptions. Candidates not recorded in detail still have only the shallow screening. An out-of-scope conclusion is relative to the current research conditions; it can be re-assessed with reasons when a per-paper check finds a new load-bearing condition. The screening method is in Case screening method and boundary. A fixed number of cases is not used to prove a contribution.

The read range is ACR-C01–C38, plus ASE-C01–C24 and ASE-A01–A09. **38+33 is the number of register units, not the number of independent incidents.** ASE-C01/C02 are collections, C10 is a source family, C11 is a benchmark, and C20/C21 are mechanism analyses. ## B. Adopted cases and load-bearing claims

The nature of each incident, source verification and the actual endpoint are recorded separately. Missing versions/configurations, dates of execution or permissions all stay unknown. Source disclosure/fix dates are not used as experiment dates. Each entry states the scope of direct verification or of reused earlier verification; the actual reading log is in §D. ### B1. ACR-C27: Credential disclosure in Cursor tool poisoning

- **Event and roles:** Invariant's product experiment; 2025-04-01 is the disclosure date. The material verified this round does not fix the actual date of execution. A is the Cursor agent doing the user's arithmetic task; B is the researcher-controlled MCP server/tool description. A is described as an induced client, not a pre-arranged colluding agent. The research environment was prepared by the researchers and cannot be treated as an in-the-wild victim.
- **Material and performer:** the user's SSH private key and MCP configuration. The configuration may contain other service credentials, but that does not mean every item was checked for validity. A read and submitted the content, so this is **P1**. The premise is that the user connected that MCP server, and that A can read files and call tools.
- **Reported endpoint:** the authors explicitly report that the file contents were sent to the malicious server. A confirmation dialog exists but did not fully show the tool input. No later SSH login, authentication to other services or impersonation of an agent instance's identity was observed.
- **Location/verification:** [Invariant, *MCP Security Notification: Tool Poisoning Attacks*](https://invariantlabs.ai/blog/mcp-security-notification), Luca Beurer-Kellner, Marc Fischer, 2025-04-01; **Experiment 1: Attacking Cursor with Tool Poisoning** (this round's HTML 60–68); the original text was verified this round. Earlier provenance: ACR raw/002 **C1 return → record 1** (including the author information and reading limits at that time).
- **Usable judgement and limits:** supports “an agent on a benign task may be induced to disclose credentials it can read”. It does not prove that those credentials belong exclusively to the agent, nor that a secret that was originally unreadable was broken into. Server sandboxing and client read permission are different conditions. This round does not analyse any mechanism paper.

### B2. ACR-C14/ASE-A08: Comet user OTP disclosure

- **Event and roles:** Brave product PoC; 2025-08-20 is the disclosure date. The material verified this round does not fix the actual date of execution. A is Comet summarizing a web page for the user; B controls user content on the page; the user already has an account and a logged-in email session. The source's setting is a trigger the user is unaware of, not collusion between the two parties. The model and browser build are not given.
- **Material/path:** the user's email address and a Perplexity one-time verification code. A read them cross-site and sent them back, so this is **P1**. The identity object is user account authentication material, not an independent agent private key or instance ID.
- **Endpoint:** the original text describes email + OTP exfiltration, and then says the attacker **can take over**. This round therefore records that the authors claim the combination can be used for takeover, and **does not record an observed actual attacker login**. The video or private traces were not verified.
- **Location/verification:** [Brave, *Agentic Browser Security: Indirect Prompt Injection in Perplexity Comet*](https://brave.com/blog/comet-prompt-injection/), Artem Chaikin, Shivan Kaul Sahib, 2025-08-20; **Attack demonstration** (81–94) and **Disclosure timeline** (114–120); the original text was verified this round. Earlier provenance: ASE-A08 verification.
- **Limits:** the timeline first says it appeared fixed, and a later update says attacks of the same kind are not fully mitigated. This is not used to claim it is still exploitable now. An existing logged-in cross-site permission is a necessary configuration; this cannot be generalized to agents without that permission.

### B3. ACR-C17/ASE-A04: Supabase fictitious token table disclosure

- **Event/roles:** a PoC on a project General Analysis built itself. The material verified this round does not fix the actual date of execution; the 2025-07-08 and 2026-09-06 below are, respectively, the date marked on the current page and the Reviewed date. B can submit support tickets. A is the developer's Cursor assistant, using `service_role` via MCP; ordinary support staff have different permissions. The data is **dummy data**, not a customer theft incident.
- **Material/path and endpoint:** a test database table represents users' refresh/integration tokens. A was induced by ticket content to write the private table's contents back into a reply the customer can see. This is a **controlled material-disclosure demonstration of P1**. It is not theft of the agent's own authentication key. There was no test of redeeming a valid token, restoring a session or impersonation.
- **Conditions:** the database role used can bypass RLS and can write to a table visible to the attacker. The conclusion is not “RLS was broken” or that the current hosted MCP behaves this way by default. A is a misled development assistant, with no pre-arranged collusion with the attacker.
- **Location/verification:** [General Analysis, *Supabase MCP can leak your entire SQL database*](https://generalanalysis.com/blog/supabase-mcp-blog), Rez Havaei, Rex Liu, Maximilian Li; current page **2025-07-08 / Reviewed 2026-09-06**; **Test Setup, Actors & Privilege Boundaries, Demo Application, Attack Flow**. This round verified the setup, material and results in the original text; this round did not rerun the demonstration. Earlier provenance: ASE-A04 verification.
- **Limits:** the source makes clear that the updated guidance is not a retest of the current product. The old ACR date of 2025-06-16 cannot be taken as the current page's date. The vendor's old response that no customer leaks were reported is reused only within the scope ASE already verified, not as a new investigation this round.

### B4. N01: OpenClaw configuration obtained by an infostealer

- **Nature and configuration:** an in-the-wild infection observation reported by Hudson Rock, published 2026-02-16. The infection date, OpenClaw build, OS, initial intrusion method and permissions are unknown. B is an infostealer on an infected host; there is no evidence it was driven by an AI agent. This is only incident metadata and **not a reason to exclude its research applicability**. On 9/28 the author made clear that attackers are not limited to agents; applicability is judged by the program's actual permissions and the protection boundaries it crosses.
- **Material and acquirer:** the original text reports that generic file grabbing obtained the OpenClaw gateway token and the device private key. The former is gateway-client authentication material; the latter is used for device pairing/signing. Acquisition was done by the malicious program, so this is **P2**. There is no report of a victim agent being induced to disclose. The source does not provide a full trace of A's behaviour, so it cannot further be claimed that A took absolutely no action from start to finish.
- **Endpoint:** the observation supports exfiltration of configuration and key material. The original text's **Technical Risk** (impersonating the client, signing or bypassing device checks) and the Enki analysis are risk explanations; **no actual successful impersonation is reported**.
- **Location/verification:** [Hudson Rock/InfoStealers, *Hudson Rock Identifies Real-World Infostealer Infection Targeting OpenClaw Configurations*](https://www.infostealers.com/article/hudson-rock-identifies-real-world-infostealer-infection-targeting-openclaw-configurations/), byline InfoStealers, 2026-02-16; the lead, **A “Grab-Bag” Attack with Targeted Impact**, **Analyzing the Payload: What Was Stolen? §1–2** (this round's body text 19, 25, 30, 38, 42); the original was verified this round. The risk statements are at 35, 43 and 54. Leaked images or originals were not accessed. This is not this project's forensics or an independent reproduction.
- **Usable scope:** this is a direct representative of acquiring agent-related identity material without the victim agent performing a disclosure. It does not fix the identity granularity of each logical agent/instance, nor prove that another agent without host file access could obtain the same. These are later applicability questions.

### B6. ASE-C12: Acquisition and API use of an application workload token

- **Nature/roles:** on 2026-06-04 Sysdig reported an observation from 05-29 and attributed B as an agent-driven operator; the victim side is a marimo application pod. For comparison, this application can be mapped to an untrusted execution environment on A's side, but the original incident is not a verified two-agent deployment. Whether the attacker is AI-driven is not an inclusion threshold.
- **Before intrusion and entry:** the report's lead explicitly says the attacker was observed exploiting CVE-2026-39987, and the IOC section also marks the exploitation source. It is not merely given as “already controlled the application”. [The official marimo advisory](https://github.com/marimo-team/marimo/security/advisories/GHSA-2679-6mx9-h9xc) (2026-04-08, Summary/Root Cause/Attack Chain) confirms: the terminal WebSocket missed an authentication check; in affected versions, EDIT mode and deployments that support the terminal, an unauthenticated network-reachable attacker can get an application-side shell. The advisory lists affected versions ≤0.20.4 and the fixed version 0.23.0. **The advisory proves the entry mechanism. The attribution of the 5/29 incident still comes from Sysdig; the advisory PoC is not pieced into that incident's log.**
- **Material/path and result:** the Kubernetes service-account token mounted in the application pod was read directly by attack-side code and then used against the cluster API. It does not depend on the victim agent actively disclosing, so this is **P2**. Phase 2 shows listing the Secrets of the default namespace, which goes beyond merely discovering the token. The introduction's cluster-wide scope is not adopted, and later cloud/database logins are not filled in.
- **Pod and read capability:** an “application pod” is the Kubernetes deployment unit that runs the application and contains one or more containers. A pod is neither the application process nor the host machine. In this case the token was mounted as a file the application environment can access. The definition in the [Kubernetes Pod documentation](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/pods/) and “Assign a ServiceAccount to a Pod” in the [Service Accounts documentation](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/security/service-accounts/) describe this structure (read on 9/29; used only as mechanism explanation, not to back-fill the incident's configuration). In this case read capability was obtained through a shell, but “full command execution is required” is not a general necessary condition for leaking the file. From the file access mechanism one can derive that a narrower vulnerability able to read that file might suffice. This case did not observe such an alternative entry.
- **“Reading directly” depends on where the code runs:** if malicious code already runs inside A's application permission domain, can see the mount and has read permission, it can read the file directly. The Pod does not apply another isolation based on whether the code is benign. If the code is only in B's own Pod/container, reading the same-named path does not automatically access A's file. That needs a shared mount, an exploitable file-read interface, the ability to execute inside A, or another cross-domain permission. This case reports reading after entry through the application vulnerability, not “blocked by the Pod after entry”. This is a permission explanation based on [the description of the container file-system view in Kubernetes Volumes](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/storage/volumes/#background). It does not claim that all Pods are necessarily well isolated or certainly have no shared storage.
- **Two effect paths, not to be forced into one chain:** the entry is external exploitation of marimo. After application control, the report separately describes ① reachable Docker management socket → start a privileged container → get host access; ② read the token mounted in the application pod → call the Kubernetes API. ① lands in high host privilege, which this thesis excludes. For ②, the material was already in the application environment; because the report writes Phase 1 first, one cannot conclude that ② must depend on ①. What is split here is the causal dependency. It does not claim that the two paths actually ran concurrently, or that ② has been reproduced independently in a low-privilege configuration.
- **The gap that really decides the scope of impact:** what is known is “the report confirms the token was read and used”. What is unknown is “whether the reading process could do this with only the ordinary application permissions to be compared”. The UID is the OS user identity of that process; together with file read permission, mount location and extra capabilities, it decides what can be read. Logs need not be filled in for the number itself. The main text does not give enough of this configuration, and the same incident did include ①, so ② cannot be upgraded to a verified restricted-B path. The official PoC's container uid=0 is not the 5/29 configuration, and is not automatically host root. If the material was already readable under ordinary application permissions, host root is not needed first; this is a conditional derivation, not a new observation from the incident.
- **Other source limits:** the main text does not provide the full entry request, the deployment or the observation environment configuration. Canaries planted by the researchers are not enough to judge whether all outputs were real production secrets or test material, so the evidence level stays at “observation reported by Sysdig”. This does not negate the external exploitation and token use it explicitly reports.
- **Use for this thesis:** service-account tokens are exactly a relevant protected object. That the credential is not called an agent ID, and that the victim side is an application, are not reasons for exclusion. It supports the condition evidence that “application control can turn into acquisition and use of reachable execution credentials”. Mapped to this thesis, one should check whether equivalent application-side permissions can read engine-held credentials, or trigger proxy use beyond their authority; no defence ruling has been made yet. It still does not independently prove that the local engine misidentifies B as A; that is another claim to be verified. Definitions and boundaries are in the mechanism description and the authoritative boundary.
- **Location/verification:** [Sysdig original report](https://www.sysdig.com/blog/agentic-threat-actor-hits-the-orchestration-plane-ai-agent-driven-container-escape), Michael Clark, 2026-06-04; lead, The agentic signature, Phase 1, Phase 2, Indicators of compromise. On 2026-09-29 the body text was rechecked, and the mechanism/conditions/reproduction environment of the official advisory above were additionally read; only source analysis, no attack was run. Earlier provenance: ASE-C12 verification.
